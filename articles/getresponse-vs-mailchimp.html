GetResponse vs Mailchimp: How to Compare Them Properly

Both platforms send email well. The real difference is what each one assumes you are trying to build—so compare the assumptions, not the feature counts.

What this comparison does and does not do

This is a framework, not a scoreboard. We do not quote prices, contact limits, or free-plan caps, because both providers change them and a stale number is worse than no number. What we can do is tell you which questions separate the two, so that 20 minutes on their pricing pages gives you an answer instead of a headache.

Official pages: GetResponse and Mailchimp.

The underlying difference

Mailchimp grew up as an email marketing platform and expanded outward into a broader marketing suite. Its template library, editor, and general-audience documentation are its strongest assets, and most freelancers and clients have seen its interface before.

GetResponse grew up around the full funnel: email plus landing pages, forms, and conversion funnels in the same product, with webinar functionality in some tiers. If you do not have a website that converts, that bundling removes a tool from your stack.

Neither assumption is better. The question is which one matches what you are missing.

Check 1: Does your stack already have landing pages?

If you already run a site you are happy with—WordPress, Webflow, a static site, anything—then a bundled page builder is a tier you are paying for and will not use. The comparison then comes down to the email editor, automation, and deliverability support.

If you have no site, or a site that does not convert, then a platform that gives you pages, forms, and email in one account is genuinely simpler. One login, one list, one place where the data lives.

Check 2: How complex is your automation?

Write out your intended automation before you compare. For most solo businesses it is:

  1. Someone subscribes from a form.
  2. They get a welcome email immediately.
  3. Two or three follow-ups over a week or two.
  4. They get tagged based on what they clicked.
  5. They stop receiving the sequence once they buy or reply.

That is the real test. Open the automation builder in each platform and try to build exactly that. Then check which tier includes the pieces you just used—this is where published plan tables matter most, and where people get surprised later.

Check 3: How do they count your contacts?

Ask the same three questions of both platforms, and get the answer from the provider's own documentation or support, not from a review site:

  • Do unsubscribed contacts count toward my limit?
  • Do the same people on multiple lists count once or twice?
  • What happens on the day I exceed my tier—blocked sends, automatic upgrade, or a grace period?

The answers can change the effective cost more than the advertised difference between the two platforms.

Check 4: Exit cost

Before you commit, confirm that you can export your contacts with their tags and export your email content in a usable form. A platform you can leave is a platform you can safely join. Both providers document data export; read it while you still have no data in there.

Check 5: Who will touch this account?

If a client, a VA, or a future contractor will log in, interface familiarity has real value. Mailchimp's interface is the one most people have already seen. If you are the only operator, this carries no weight at all.

How to decide

Choose GetResponse if you need landing pages and email in one place and want fewer tools. Choose Mailchimp if you already have a site, value a large template library, and want the interface other people already know. If both pass your test equally, pick the cheaper one at the contact count you expect in 12 months—not today's.

Verify all plan features, limits, and prices on the official GetResponse and Mailchimp websites before subscribing. This article describes a comparison method and does not state current commercial terms.
