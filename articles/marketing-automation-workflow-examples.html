5 Marketing Automation Workflows a One-Person Business Can Actually Run

Most automation tutorials describe systems built for a marketing team. These five workflows are small enough that one person can build, maintain, and debug them.

How to read these

Each workflow is written as trigger, steps, and exit condition. The exit condition is the part people forget, and it is the part that stops a customer receiving a sales sequence after they have already bought.

Every mainstream email platform with automation can build these — GetResponse, Mailchimp, MailerLite, Kit and Brevo all document their automation builders publicly. If a platform cannot build the five below, that tells you something useful about the platform.

Workflow 1: The welcome sequence

Trigger: a new subscriber from your signup form.

  1. Immediately: deliver whatever you promised, plus one line on who you help.
  2. Day 2: one concrete, useful tip related to that download. No pitch.
  3. Day 5: a short case description—problem, what you did, outcome.
  4. Day 8: what working with you looks like and how to start.

Exit condition: they reply, book a call, or buy. Any of those should stop the sequence immediately.

Why it is first: it runs once per subscriber forever and costs you nothing after the afternoon you write it.

Workflow 2: The enquiry that went quiet

Trigger: a contact tagged enquired with no booking after 7 days.

  1. Day 7: a two-line email—"still thinking about X? happy to answer anything."
  2. Day 14: one relevant example of similar work.
  3. Day 30: a final short note, then remove the tag.

Exit condition: they reply or book. Remove the tag so they never enter again.

Why it works: most enquiries go quiet because the person got busy, not because they chose someone else. Two polite follow-ups recover more work than any amount of new traffic.

Workflow 3: Post-delivery follow-up

Trigger: a contact tagged delivered.

  1. Day 3: check everything landed well. One question, easy to answer.
  2. Day 14: ask for a testimonial or a referral—one specific ask, not both.
  3. Day 90: a light check-in with one useful thing, no pitch.

Exit condition: none needed, but keep it slow and stop if they unsubscribe.

Why it matters: repeat and referral work is the cheapest revenue a solo business has, and it is almost always lost to forgetting rather than to dissatisfaction.

Workflow 4: The availability notice

Trigger: manual, when you have capacity.

  1. Segment: past clients plus anyone tagged enquired in the last 12 months.
  2. Send one short email: what you have availability for, when, and how to claim it.
  3. Tag everyone who replies.

Exit condition: you are booked. Stop sending.

Why it works: it is the only "campaign" in this list with a direct revenue line, and it takes ten minutes once the segment exists.

Workflow 5: Re-engagement and list hygiene

Trigger: no email interaction in 6 to 12 months.

  1. Send one email that asks plainly whether they still want to hear from you.
  2. If no response after 14 days, move them to an inactive segment and stop sending.

Exit condition: any interaction returns them to the main list.

Why it matters: inactive contacts hurt deliverability and, on many platforms, still count toward your paid contact limit. This workflow improves your results and can lower your bill at the same time.

Build order

Build workflow 1 first, then 3, then 2. Workflows 4 and 5 are manual or annual and can wait. Do not build all five in one sitting: each one needs a week of real traffic before you trust it.

Before you switch any of them on

  • Send yourself through the whole sequence as a test contact.
  • Check every link, on a phone and on a desktop.
  • Confirm the exit conditions fire. This is where silent failures live.
  • Make sure the unsubscribe link works and that unsubscribing removes them from every sequence, not just one.
Automation features differ by platform and plan tier. Verify that the triggers and conditions you need are included in your tier on the provider's official pricing page before you commit.
