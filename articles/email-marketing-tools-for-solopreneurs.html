How to Choose an Email Marketing Tool as a Solopreneur

Most "best email tool" lists rank products you will never use. This guide works the other way around: define your own constraints first, then let them eliminate most of the market for you.

Why most comparison lists do not help

Email platforms look interchangeable on a feature grid. In practice the decision is driven by four or five constraints specific to you: how many contacts you have, whether you sell a product or a service, whether you need automation now or later, and how much time you are willing to spend on setup. Once those are written down, most of the market disqualifies itself.

Work through the questions below and write your answers somewhere. The answers are your requirements document.

Question 1: How many contacts will you have in 12 months?

Nearly every provider prices by contact count, subscriber count, or monthly sends. The jump between tiers is usually where the real cost sits, not the entry price.

  • Under a few hundred contacts: almost any provider works, so optimise for ease of setup.
  • Low thousands: start comparing the cost of the next tier up, not the current one.
  • Growing fast: check whether unsubscribed or inactive contacts still count toward your limit. This single rule changes the effective price more than any discount.

Confirm the current tiers on the provider's own pricing page before you commit. Published prices and contact bands change, and regional pricing differs.

Question 2: Are you selling a product or your time?

If you sell your time—consulting, design, coaching—your list is small and high value. You need good one-to-one deliverability, simple segmentation, and a reliable booking or reply path. Heavy e-commerce automation is wasted.

If you sell a product or a course, the automation matters: abandoned-cart style sequences, purchase tagging, and post-sale follow-up. Here a cheap tool without automation becomes expensive in manual work.

Question 3: Do you need a landing page too?

Some email platforms include forms, landing pages, and simple funnels. Others expect you to bring your own site. If you do not already have a website that converts, a platform with built-in pages removes a whole dependency. If you have a site you like, that bundled builder is a feature you will pay for and never open.

Question 4: How much automation do you actually need on day one?

Be honest. A welcome sequence of three to five emails covers most solo businesses for the first year. If a platform makes that easy and the visual workflow builder is locked behind a higher tier, that may be fine for now—as long as you can upgrade later without migrating.

Ask of any candidate: can I export my contacts, and can I export my email content? If both answers are yes, a wrong choice is recoverable.

Question 5: What happens when something breaks?

Deliverability problems and billing problems are the two failures that cost real money. Before signing up, check what support channel your tier includes, and whether the provider documents its sending limits and compliance requirements clearly. A provider that is vague about compliance is a provider that will be vague when your sends stop.

How to run a two-week test

  1. Pick two platforms, not five.
  2. Import 20 real contacts, not a test list.
  3. Build the same welcome email in both. Time yourself.
  4. Send to yourself on desktop and phone, and check how the email renders in both.
  5. Build one two-step automation in each.
  6. Try to export your contacts and content. If that is hard, stop there.
  7. Choose the one that took less of your time. Feature depth you cannot reach is not an advantage.
Nothing in this article is a recommendation to buy a specific plan. Verify current features, limits, and prices on the provider's official website before you subscribe.
