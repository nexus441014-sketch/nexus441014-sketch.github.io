Riding Trains in Japan: The Bits Nobody Explains

The trains are easy. The things that trip people up are the ticket categories, the difference between operators, and station exits. All three are learnable in one read.

The one concept that explains most confusion

A Japanese train fare is often two charges, not one: a basic fare for the distance, plus a surcharge for the kind of train you used. Express, limited express and shinkansen services all add a surcharge. Local trains do not.

This is why a ticket machine sometimes gives you two tickets for one journey, and why tapping an IC card does not work on a limited express. You paid the distance, not the speed.

Reserved, non-reserved, green

On shinkansen and limited express services there are usually three choices.

TypeWhat it meansWhen to pick it
Non-reserved (自由席)Any seat in designated cars, first comeShort hops, flexible timing, off-peak
Reserved (指定席)A specific seat on a specific trainPeak periods, large luggage, groups
Green car (グリーン車)Reserved, more space, extra chargeLong legs, or when you want quiet

Reserve if you are travelling with big suitcases, on a holiday weekend, or as a group. Otherwise non-reserved is genuinely fine and lets you take the next train if you are running late. Reserving costs nothing extra on most passes.

Note that on some shinkansen routes, oversized luggage now requires a specific reserved space booked in advance. If your case is at the large end, ask at the window rather than guessing.

Why your app shows trains your ticket cannot use

Map apps show the fastest route regardless of ticket type. If you hold a Japan Rail Pass, the fastest Tokyo–Osaka services are the ones the pass excludes, so the app and your ticket disagree.

The fix is to set the app to avoid the excluded service, or use a Japan-specific route planner that knows about pass validity. This single mismatch causes more pass-holder confusion than anything else — see do you need a Japan Rail Pass for whether a pass is even worth it for your route.

Different companies, same station

A big station can host a national operator, two or three private railways, and a municipal subway. They have separate ticket gates, separate fares, and sometimes separate buildings under one name.

Practical consequences:

  • Transferring between operators means exiting one gate and entering another. Your

journey is two fares, not one.

  • An IC card handles this invisibly, which is most of why it is worth having. See

IC cards explained.

  • "Shinjuku Station" can mean a twenty-minute walk from one end to the other.

Exits matter more than platforms

In Japanese cities, the exit number is the address. Coming out of the wrong side of a large station can mean a fifteen-minute walk back around, sometimes with no way to cross.

Before you tap out, check the exit. Map apps give the exit number; signage inside the station is bilingual and leads you there. This habit saves more time than any other on this page.

Rush hour is real

Roughly 7:30–9:00 in the morning on urban lines, and 17:30–19:30 in the evening. Not dangerous, but genuinely uncomfortable with luggage, and the one time you should avoid moving between hotels.

If you must travel with bags at those times, use an off-peak gap or forward your luggage instead.

Unwritten rules that are actually observed

  • Queue on the platform markings. They show where the doors will be.
  • Let people off first. Stand aside, don't block the door.
  • Phone on silent; no calls. Texting is fine.
  • Backpack off your back and held low, or on the rack, when it is busy.
  • Priority seats near the doors: give them up without being asked.
  • Eating is fine on long-distance trains, not on commuter ones.

When you get it wrong

Nearly every mistake is fixable at a fare adjustment machine near the gates, or at the staffed window beside them.

  • Underpaid? Insert the ticket at the fare adjustment machine and pay the difference.
  • IC card short of balance? Top it up at the same machine before exiting.
  • Wrong train? Get off at the next stop and ride back; no penalty on the basic fare.
  • Lost ticket? Go to the staffed window. Expect to pay, and expect to be treated

politely.

  • Missed a reserved train? On many services you can take a non-reserved seat on a later

one the same day. Ask rather than assume.

Staff at the window are the answer to almost everything, and they deal with this all day.

If a pass turns out to be worth it

For routes where the long-distance legs stack up, passes and regional tickets can be bought before you fly, which avoids queueing at a counter on arrival. Klook lists the national pass next to the regional ones, which is the comparison that actually decides it — most trips are better served by a regional pass than the national one.

Work out your route first, then price it. Buying a pass and then designing a trip to justify it is how people end up on trains they did not want to be on.
