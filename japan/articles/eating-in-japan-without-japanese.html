Eating in Japan Without Speaking Japanese

Japan is one of the easiest countries in the world to eat well in without the language, once you know how the systems work. The anxiety comes almost entirely from three or four specific situations.

The country is built for people who cannot read the menu

Plastic food models in the window. Photographs on the menu. Laminated English menus kept under the counter for anyone who looks foreign. Ticket machines with pictures. None of this exists for tourists — it exists because Japanese restaurants have always competed on making the choice obvious — but it works in your favour.

The practical consequence: you can point at things and it is normal. Pointing at a plastic model in the window, or at a photo on a menu, is how a lot of ordering happens here, including between Japanese speakers.

Ticket machines, the thing that stops people

A vending machine by the door, covered in buttons, with a queue behind you. This is the single most common moment a visitor freezes and walks out of a restaurant they wanted to eat at.

How it works:

  1. Put the money in first. The buttons stay dark until the machine has your cash. This

is the step people miss — they press buttons at a machine with no money in it, nothing happens, and they conclude the machine is broken.

  1. Press the button for your dish. The top-left area is usually the shop's signature

dish. Many machines have photos; the ones that do not have the dish name in Japanese only, which is where a translation app earns its place.

  1. Take the ticket and your change. Some machines need you to press a change lever.
  2. Sit down and put the ticket on the counter. Staff will collect it. You do not need

to say anything.

Some machines take IC cards, which removes the cash step entirely — another reason to set one up on day one, as in IC cards in Japan explained.

Getting seated

Wait just inside the door rather than seating yourself. Someone will come, hold up fingers or ask how many — hold up fingers back. That is the entire interaction.

At a counter you may be seated next to strangers. This is normal and not a sign that something has gone wrong.

Ordering

Point. At the photo, at the model, at the item on the menu. Adding kore o kudasai ("this one please") makes it complete, but pointing alone works.

Ask for English. Eigo no menyū arimasu ka? Many places have one. In chains, the touchscreen tablet on the table usually has a language button in the corner.

Call staff rather than waiting to be noticed. Say sumimasen across the room — this is polite, not rude. Many restaurants have a call button on the table instead.

The cover charge that looks like a scam

At an izakaya — a drinking restaurant — a small dish you did not order may arrive shortly after you sit down. This is otōshi, and it is a seat charge rather than a gift. It appears on the bill as a few hundred yen per person.

It is not a scam, it is not a mistake, and it is not negotiable in most places. Knowing it is coming removes the only unpleasant surprise most visitors meet in a Japanese restaurant. If you are eating somewhere that serves alcohol in the evening, expect it.

Paying

Pay at the register, not the table. Take the slip of paper left on your table to the counter by the door. Waving a card at your table will usually get you a confused look.

A small tray on the counter is where money goes, rather than into the cashier's hand. Cards go on it too.

There is no tipping. None. Leaving money on the table means someone will chase you down the street to return it.

Many small, excellent restaurants are cash only. See cash or card in Japan.

Convenience stores are genuinely good

This surprises people more than anything else on this page. A Japanese convenience store sells rice balls, sandwiches, noodle dishes, hot food from a counter by the register, and decent coffee, at a quality that has no equivalent in most countries.

Practicalities: staff will ask whether to heat your food — nodding is a complete answer. Chopsticks and a spoon are offered or included. There is usually a bin and a small eating counter in or near larger branches. Eating while walking down the street is mildly frowned upon; eating outside the shop is fine.

A convenience store breakfast is not a compromise. It is what a lot of people here eat.

Chains worth knowing

When you are tired, indecisive, or feeding someone fussy, the chains solve it: beef bowl shops, standing soba counters, curry houses, conveyor-belt sushi, family restaurants with photo menus and tablets. All are cheap, fast, consistent, and designed to be used without conversation.

Using them is not failing at travelling in Japan. It is what residents do on a Tuesday.

If you have dietary restrictions

Be aware of one structural problem: dashi, the stock underlying an enormous amount of Japanese cooking, is usually made with bonito — dried fish. Dishes that look vegetarian frequently are not, and staff may answer "no meat" truthfully while the dish contains fish stock.

Say what you cannot eat rather than naming a category. "I cannot eat meat, fish or fish stock" gets further than "I am vegetarian", which may be understood as "no red meat". Writing it down in Japanese on your phone before the trip is worth the ten minutes.

Halal and strict vegan options exist in large cities but need looking up in advance rather than found on the day.

When a food tour earns its price

Most meals do not need a guide. The case for one is the same as for any tour: it buys you access or explanation you cannot get alone. A food tour is worth it when it takes you into places with no English path in, or when you want the context behind what you are eating on the first night rather than working it out over a week.

Klook and GetYourGuide both list food tours in the major cities. Sort by review count rather than position, and check the same tour on both. The fuller argument is in when a day tour in Japan is worth it.

Five phrases that cover most of it

  • Sumimasen — excuse me, and also sorry, and also thank you. The most useful word here.
  • Kore o kudasai — this one, please.
  • Oishii — delicious. Say it to the person who cooked it.
  • Okaikei onegaishimasu — the bill, please.
  • Gochisōsama deshita — said on leaving. Roughly "thank you for the meal".

None of these are required. All of them make the exchange warmer.
