eSIM, Pocket WiFi or a SIM Card in Japan: How to Decide

Getting online is the first thing you will need in Japan and the easiest thing to overbuy. The right answer depends on three things about your trip, not on which option is "best".

Why this is the first decision

Almost everything else in a Japan trip assumes you are online: train routing, map navigation on foot, translating a menu, and increasingly the official services at the airport. Public WiFi exists but is patchy, often requires a registration flow, and is not something to rely on while standing in a station concourse with luggage.

You have three realistic options. None of them is universally correct.

The three questions that decide it

1. How many people are travelling together?

One person: an eSIM is usually the simplest. Three or more sharing: a pocket WiFi router starts to make sense, because one rental covers everyone.

2. Does your phone support eSIM, and is it carrier-unlocked?

This is the question people skip, and it is the only one that can leave you with nothing working on arrival. Check both before you buy anything. A phone still locked to a home carrier cannot use a physical Japanese SIM either.

3. Do you need a Japanese phone number?

Most visitors do not. Data alone covers maps, messaging apps and calls over the internet. You need an actual number only for things like some restaurant reservation systems or a domestic delivery contact. If you do need one, that rules out most data-only eSIMs.

Before you buy, check these five things

  1. Coverage area. Urban Japan is well covered by all the major networks. Mountain and

rural areas are not uniform. If your itinerary leaves the main corridors, check which network the plan actually runs on.

  1. What happens when you hit the data cap. Some plans throttle to an unusable speed,

some stop entirely, some let you top up. This matters more than the headline allowance.

  1. Activation timing. Some eSIMs activate on first connection, others on a fixed date.

Getting this wrong wastes the first day.

  1. Tethering. If you plan to share with a travelling companion or a laptop, confirm

tethering is permitted. Not all plans allow it.

  1. Return logistics for rented routers. A pocket WiFi you cannot return at your

departure airport becomes an expensive problem. Confirm the return point and deadline before you collect it.

How much data do you actually need

Maps, messaging and web use are modest. The consumption comes from video, photo backup and video calls. Two practical habits make a small plan comfortable:

  • Turn off automatic cloud photo backup for the trip, or restrict it to WiFi.
  • Download offline maps for the cities you will walk in before you leave home.

With those two changes, most sightseeing travellers use far less than the plans marketed at them assume.

The honest default

Solo, recent unlocked phone, a week or two of normal sightseeing: a modest data-only eSIM, bought before departure, activated on landing. Group of three or more, or any doubt about phone compatibility: a pocket WiFi router collected at the airport.

If you are staying more than a month, neither is the cheapest answer and a physical SIM or a local plan deserves a proper look.
