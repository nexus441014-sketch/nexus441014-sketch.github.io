When to Go to Japan, and What to Pack

Japan's seasons are more extreme than the photographs suggest, and the packing list that matters here is short and specific. Most of it is not what travel checklists tell you.

The seasons are not mild

The photographs that sell Japan are taken in two short windows. The rest of the year is more extreme than visitors expect.

Summer is the one people get wrong. It is not "warm". Much of the country is hot and very humid for weeks at a time, to a degree that changes what you can realistically do in a day. Heatstroke is a genuine seasonal health problem here, not a traveller's exaggeration. If you come in high summer, plan indoor activities for the middle of the day and carry water, as residents do.

The rainy season runs roughly from June into July across most of the country, earlier in Okinawa. Hokkaido is generally considered not to have a distinct one, which makes it a sensible destination in that window. It is not constant rain — it is humidity, grey skies and sudden downpours.

Typhoon season peaks in late summer and early autumn. The practical risk is not danger so much as disruption: flights cancelled, trains suspended, outdoor plans lost. If your dates fall in it, keep some slack in the itinerary and avoid non-refundable bookings for the back half of the trip.

Winter divides sharply. Tokyo and the Pacific side are cold, dry and often sunny. The Sea of Japan side gets some of the heaviest snowfall in the world. The same week can be two completely different trips depending on which side of the mountains you are on.

Spring and autumn are genuinely lovely and genuinely crowded. This is the trade.

Choosing your dates

If the dates are yours to choose, late spring or autumn, just outside the peak weeks, gives you the best combination of weather and space.

Two things to check before fixing anything:

  • The domestic holiday weeks, when the whole country travels at once. Accommodation

does not merely get expensive, it runs out.

  • Whether you are planning around the cherry blossom. The dates move year to year and

are forecast only weeks ahead. Booking a trip months in advance around a specific blossom week is a gamble. Treat it as a bonus rather than the plan.

For the colours in autumn, the front moves south over several weeks, which makes it easier to catch than the blossom.

What to pack that you would not think of

Shoes you can take off easily. You will remove them more often than you expect: some restaurants, temple interiors, ryokan, changing rooms, and anywhere with a raised wooden floor. Laces you have to kneel to undo become a small daily irritation. Clean socks without holes, for the same reason.

A small towel or handkerchief. Many public toilets have neither a dryer nor paper towels. Everyone here carries one. It is also the towel you will want at an onsen.

Your own deodorant if you rely on a strong one. Many visitors find what is sold here milder than they are used to.

Layers in winter, because the indoor situation is inverted from what you may expect: trains, shops and restaurants are heated hard, while older buildings and some ryokan are heated room by room rather than centrally. You will be peeling layers off on the train and putting them back on in your room.

Shoes, if you take a large size. Ordinary Japanese shops stock a narrower size range than you may be used to. Easier to bring what you need than to shop for it.

A plug adapter for Type A, two flat pins, 100V. Most modern electronics handle the voltage, but check anything with a motor or heating element.

Medication: check this before you fly

Japan regulates what you may bring in, and some medications that are ordinary elsewhere — including certain cold and allergy remedies and some prescription stimulants — are restricted or prohibited. Bringing more than a limited supply of prescription medicine can require an Import Confirmation obtained in advance.

This is the one item on this page where getting it wrong has consequences at the border rather than merely being inconvenient. Check your specific medication against the official guidance before you book, not the week before you fly:

Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare — bringing medicines for personal use into Japan

Keep medicines in their original labelled packaging, and carry a copy of the prescription.

What to leave at home

An umbrella. Convenience stores sell them everywhere for very little, the moment it starts raining.

Most toiletries. Convenience stores and drugstores are dense and cheap.

Too many clothes. Coin laundries are everywhere, and many hotels have machines. Pack for four or five days and wash.

A very large suitcase. Japanese stations, trains and hotel rooms are not built around them. If you are moving between cities, a case you can lift onto a rack with one hand changes the trip — and anything bigger is better sent ahead than dragged through a rush-hour interchange.

The short version

Come in late spring or autumn if you can choose. Avoid the domestic holiday weeks. Pack shoes you can slip off, a small towel, layers, and a case you can carry. Check your medication against the official rules before anything else on this list.

The two decisions this one feeds into

Once you know the season, the next question is length. The number of days matters less than the number of places you unpack in, which is the argument in how many days do you need in Japan.

The packing list above also leans on one thing worth settling before you fly: some ordinary Western medications are restricted here, and the check takes minutes. If you get sick in Japan covers that and the pharmacy system.
