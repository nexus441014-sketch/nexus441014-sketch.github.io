Do You Need to Speak Japanese in Japan?

No, and the places people worry about are rarely the places it matters. The gap is not vocabulary — it is knowing which situations have a script and which do not.

The honest answer

You do not need Japanese to travel in Japan. Signage on major train lines, airports and tourist sites is bilingual, ticket machines have an English button, and a phone camera translates a menu in seconds.

What you do need is tolerance for a specific kind of awkwardness: many people you deal with will understand more English than they are comfortable speaking, and will apologise for it. That is not hostility. It is embarrassment, and it goes away if you slow down.

Where English is genuinely fine

  • Airports and major stations — bilingual signage, English announcements, staff used to

visitors

  • Chain hotels and anywhere with online booking — the person at the desk has done this

before

  • Convenience stores — the whole transaction is a scanner and a screen
  • Big-city museums, department stores, electronics shops — often multilingual staff
  • Ticket machines and ATMs — look for the English button first, it is almost always

there

Where it actually gets hard

These are the real pinch points, and none of them is a restaurant menu.

The pattern: anything that goes wrong is harder than anything that goes right. Routine transactions are designed to need no language at all.

The phrases that actually earn their keep

Not a vocabulary list. These are the six that change outcomes.

  1. Sumimasen — "excuse me" and "sorry" and "hello, I need help". The single most useful

word in the language.

  1. Arigatō gozaimasu — thank you, politely. Use it constantly.
  2. Eigo no menyū arimasu ka? — "Do you have an English menu?"
  3. Kore o kudasai — "This one, please", while pointing. Solves most ordering.
  4. Toire wa doko desu ka? — "Where is the toilet?"
  5. Daijōbu desu — "I'm fine / no thank you / it's okay". Declines things gracefully.

Learning to read two characters also pays off disproportionately: 女 (women) and 男 (men), for changing rooms and toilets. Colours on curtains are not reliable.

Make the phone do the work

Translation apps are good enough now that the camera mode is the single biggest improvement to your trip. Two things to do before you fly:

  • Download the Japanese offline language pack in your translation app. Station

basements and some restaurants have no usable signal.

  • Learn the camera mode, not just the text box. Pointing it at a menu, a label or a

sign is where you will use it 90% of the time.

Say the destination, don't spell it

The most common failure is a place name. English romanisation is not always what a driver or shopkeeper recognises, and pronunciation guesses often miss.

Show, don't say. Keep the destination in Japanese characters on your phone — screenshot it from a map app before you leave the hotel wifi. This one habit removes most taxi and directions problems.

Restaurants: the thing people fear most and need least

Ordering is the easiest part of Japan, not the hardest. Ticket machines, photo menus, plastic food models, pointing, and the conveyor belt all exist precisely so that nobody has to talk. Eating in Japan without Japanese walks through each type.

The restaurants where language genuinely matters are small counter places with a set menu and a chef who talks to you. Those are worth doing — but save them for later in the trip, not the first jet-lagged night.

What changes outside the big cities

The gap widens, but less than you would think. Rural Japan sees fewer foreign visitors, so staff have less practice — yet the same tools still work, and people tend to put more effort into helping, not less.

Two practical adjustments: carry your accommodation's address in Japanese, and do not assume a small station has a staffed window late in the evening.

If you want to learn something before you go

Spending a week on hiragana and katakana is better value than a week on phrases. Katakana in particular is mostly foreign loanwords, so being able to sound it out turns a lot of menus and shop signs into words you already know — コーヒー is coffee, ビール is beer, チケット is ticket.

Everything beyond that is optional. Japan is built to be navigated without the language; the small effort just makes it friendlier.
