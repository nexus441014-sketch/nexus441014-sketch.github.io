Vegetarian, Vegan and Halal in Japan: What to Watch For

Japan is harder than it should be for restricted diets, and the reason is structural rather than cultural indifference. Once you understand the one ingredient at the centre of it, most of the confusion resolves.

The one ingredient behind most of the problem

Dashi. It is the stock underneath an enormous proportion of Japanese cooking, and it is usually made with katsuobushi — dried, smoked, fermented bonito. Fish.

This is not an occasional garnish. It is the base layer of miso soup, noodle broths, simmered vegetables, dipping sauces, egg dishes and a great deal else. A dish can contain no visible animal product and still be built on fish stock.

A version made only from kombu seaweed exists and is entirely plant-based. It is simply not the default.

The practical consequence: "no meat" and "vegetarian" are not the same conversation here. Staff answering "yes, no meat" are often answering honestly about a dish that contains fish stock.

Dishes that look safe and usually are not

  • Miso soup — almost always fish dashi.
  • Vegetable tempura — the batter may be fine, but the dipping sauce (tentsuyu) is

dashi-based, and the oil is shared with prawns and fish.

  • Soba and udon — the noodles are usually fine; the broth and the dipping sauce are

dashi.

  • Ramen — broth is pork, chicken or seafood. A "vegetable ramen" often means vegetable

toppings on an animal broth. Dedicated vegan ramen shops exist in large cities and are worth seeking out, but do not assume.

  • Japanese curry — the roux commonly contains animal fats.
  • Simmered vegetable side dishes — the simmering liquid is dashi.
  • Salads — dressings frequently contain fish or dairy derivatives.
  • Tamagoyaki — the sweet rolled omelette usually has dashi in it.

What is more reliably fine

  • Shōjin ryōri, Buddhist temple cuisine, is the one genuinely reliable Japanese

tradition here: no animal products at all. Note that it also traditionally excludes onion and garlic, which some people find unexpectedly plain. Served at some temples and specialist restaurants, usually needs booking.

  • Indian restaurants, which are everywhere in Japan and well used to the question.
  • Dedicated vegan and vegetarian restaurants in Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka and other large

cities. Plentiful if you look them up; essentially absent in small towns.

  • Convenience store items you have read the label on — see below.
  • Kappa-maki (cucumber roll) and plain rice dishes, with the caveat that sushi rice

seasoning is usually fine but worth asking about.

Reading a label in a convenience store

Convenience stores list ingredients on every package, which makes them the most controllable option in the country. Photograph the ingredients panel and run it through a translation app.

Saying it so that it works

Do not lead with the category. "I am vegetarian" may be heard as "no red meat", and the word has no settled meaning in everyday Japanese usage.

Lead with the list instead:

肉・魚・だし（かつお）が食べられません I cannot eat meat, fish, or dashi (bonito).

For a stricter diet:

動物性のものは食べられません。だし・卵・乳製品も含みます I cannot eat anything from animals, including dashi, eggs and dairy.

Write it down before you travel. A card on your phone, in Japanese, that you can show is worth far more than attempting it aloud. Staff can read it, take it to the kitchen, and give you a real answer.

Ask before you sit down where you can. Being told after ordering is the common failure.

Halal specifically

Two separate problems, and the second is the one people underestimate.

Pork is widely used, including in places it is not obvious: broths, gyoza fillings, and lard as a cooking fat.

Alcohol is in the seasonings. Mirin and cooking sake are standard in Japanese cooking, and brewed soy sauce contains a small amount of alcohol. A dish with no meat at all may still not be acceptable depending on how strictly you observe.

Certified halal restaurants exist in the major cities and near large mosques, and some have prayer space. Outside those areas, self-catering and reading labels becomes the realistic approach. Certification is displayed at the restaurant; where it matters to you, look for it rather than relying on a menu description.

Where it is easy and where it is not

Easy: central Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka. Enough dedicated restaurants that you can eat well without compromise, if you look them up in advance.

Hard: small towns, mountain areas, and traditional inns. A ryokan dinner is a fixed multi-course meal built around what is local and seasonal, which very often means fish. Many will accommodate a restriction if told well in advance — at booking, not at check-in. Some genuinely cannot. Ask before you book, and see where to stay in Japan for how ryokan meals work.

This is one of the things worth sorting before you fly rather than on the day: see what you need to book before you arrive.

The honest summary

Japan is not hostile to restricted diets, but it is not built around them either, and the gap between "looks vegetarian" and "is vegetarian" is wider here than in most countries. With a written card, a label-reading habit and a list of restaurants saved before you arrive, you will eat well. Without those, you will eat a lot of plain rice.
