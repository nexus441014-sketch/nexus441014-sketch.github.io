If You Get Sick in Japan

Japanese healthcare is good and the pharmacies are well stocked. The two things that catch visitors out are the medicines you cannot bring in, and the fact that you will be billed in full.

Before you fly: the medication rule

This is the part that genuinely goes wrong, and it goes wrong at the border rather than at a pharmacy.

Some medicines that are ordinary elsewhere are prohibited or restricted in Japan. The best-known cases are stimulants used for ADHD, and common Western cold and sinus preparations containing pseudoephedrine. Bringing them can mean confiscation or worse, and a prescription from home does not by itself make it legal.

If you take any prescription medication regularly, check it against the Japanese Ministry of Health's rules before you book, not before you fly. Some medicines require an import certificate (Yakkan Shōmei) applied for in advance, and that takes time.

For everything else: carry it in original packaging, bring a copy of the prescription, and keep it in your hand luggage.

What you can buy over the counter

Japanese drugstores are abundant and well stocked. Without a prescription you can generally buy painkillers, cold and flu remedies, stomach medicine, antihistamines, rehydration drinks, wound dressings, motion sickness tablets and eye drops.

Two practical notes:

  • Doses are often lower than equivalent products elsewhere. Read the packet.
  • A pharmacy counter (薬局) inside or beside the drugstore handles prescriptions; the

shop floor handles everything else.

Translate the symptom before you go in, not at the counter. "Sore throat and a dry cough, no fever" typed into a translation app and shown to the pharmacist works far better than naming a brand from home.

Seeing a doctor as a visitor

The system is clinic-first. For anything non-urgent, you go to a small local clinic rather than a hospital, often without an appointment, during posted hours.

How to find one that can handle you:

  1. Ask your accommodation. Hotel desks do this routinely and will often phone ahead.
  2. Use the JNTO or prefectural medical institution search, which can be filtered for

languages spoken.

  1. Call the Japan Visitor Hotline — 050-3816-2787, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, in

English, Chinese and Korean. They handle illness and accidents as well as general tourist information.

Clinics commonly close in the middle of the day and on Sundays and Wednesdays. If it is evening, look for an emergency outpatient department rather than waiting.

You will be billed, in full

There is no reciprocal healthcare arrangement for visitors. You are not in the national insurance system, so you pay the full cost, usually on the day, often in cash.

What to expect
PaymentDue at the visit; cash is always accepted, cards sometimes not
CostClinic visits are modest; hospital admission is not
InsuranceAlmost never billed directly — you pay, then claim
PaperworkAsk for an itemised receipt and keep it

Buy travel insurance with medical cover. This is the single most important line in this article. A clinic visit for a chest infection is an inconvenience; an ambulance, admission and a changed flight is the thing insurance exists for.

Keep a photo of your policy number and the insurer's emergency line on your phone, not only in your email.

Emergencies

  • 119 — ambulance and fire. Say "kyūkyūsha" (ambulance) and the address.
  • 110 — police.
  • Japan Visitor Hotline — 050-3816-2787, or +81-50-3816-2787 from overseas. 24 hours,

English, Chinese and Korean.

Ambulances are free to call and will take you to a hospital that can accept you; the hospital treatment is what you are billed for. Hotel staff will call on your behalf and this is a completely normal thing to ask for.

The language problem, and how to get around it

Symptoms are the hardest thing to translate well, because the vocabulary is specific and you are not at your best.

What works:

  • Type the symptoms into a translation app before you set out, in simple short

sentences, and save the screen

  • Bring a written list of your conditions, allergies and current medications, translated

in advance — do this at home, once, and keep it on your phone

  • Phone interpretation services exist for medical settings; the visitor hotline can

connect you

  • Ask your accommodation to phone the clinic first and confirm they can see you

Do you need to speak Japanese covers the general case; medical is the one situation where preparing the words in advance genuinely matters.

The things most likely to actually happen

Not dramatic, just common:

  • Heat exhaustion in summer. Shade, water, salt, air conditioning, and stop walking.

Why summer is the real risk.

  • A cold, from long flights and crowded trains. Drugstore, early.
  • Blisters. You will walk much further than you plan to. Bring the dressings you like.
  • An upset stomach from the change in diet rather than from bad food.
  • Pollen allergy in spring, which is severe in Japan and surprises people who have

never had hay fever.

Masks are widely sold and worn without comment, which helps with all of the above.

The five-minute preparation

  1. Check every prescription medication against Japan's import rules
  2. Buy travel insurance with medical cover
  3. Save 119, 110 and 050-3816-2787 in your phone
  4. Save your policy number and insurer's emergency line as a photo
  5. Write and translate a short list of your conditions, allergies and medications

Done once, before the trip, and almost none of this will ever be needed.

Nothing here is medical or legal advice. Import rules for medication change and are enforced at the border: check your own prescriptions against the official guidance, and consult a medical professional about your own health.
