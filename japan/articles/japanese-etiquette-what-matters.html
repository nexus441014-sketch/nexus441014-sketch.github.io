Japanese Etiquette: What Actually Matters

Most etiquette advice about Japan is a mix of real rules, outdated rules, and things invented by other travel sites. Six things matter. The rest you can get wrong without anyone noticing.

The six that actually matter

If you only remember one section, remember this one.

  1. Take your shoes off where you are supposed to. Homes, ryokan, temple interiors,

some restaurants, changing rooms, and anywhere with a raised floor or a row of slippers. A step up in the floor level is the signal.

  1. Be quiet on trains. No phone calls, no speakerphone, no loud conversation. This is

the rule visitors break most and locals notice most.

  1. Do not block. Stand to one side on escalators, don't stop dead at the top of

stairs, don't stand in a doorway. Flow matters more than politeness here.

  1. Wash before you get in the bath. The shared water is not for cleaning in. Covered

properly in onsen etiquette.

  1. Take your rubbish with you. Public bins are rare. Carry a small bag; dispose of it

at your accommodation or a convenience store.

  1. Don't eat or drink while walking in a crowded place. On a park bench, fine. Weaving

through Shibuya with a coffee, less fine.

Everything below is detail.

Chopstick rules worth knowing

Two are genuinely taboo because they echo funeral rites:

  • Never stand chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice.
  • Never pass food chopstick-to-chopstick with someone else.

Beyond those: don't spear food, don't wave them around, and rest them on the holder or across the bowl rather than in it. Nobody will say anything if you hold them clumsily.

Tipping

Don't. There is no tipping culture, service charges are built in, and leaving cash on a table can cause confusion or a chase down the street to return it.

The exception is a private guide or a high-end ryokan where a gratuity in an envelope is sometimes customary — and in those cases you will be told.

Money is handed over on a tray

Most shops and restaurants have a small tray by the register. Put your cash on the tray rather than into the person's hand, and take change from it. Handing a card directly is fine.

Bowing: don't overthink it

A small nod is enough for everything you will encounter. You are not expected to bow correctly, and attempting a deep formal bow is more likely to read as odd than respectful. Nod and say arigatō gozaimasu.

Things you have been told that nobody minds

The "rule"Reality
Slurping noodles is requiredIt is accepted, not required. Eat how you like
Never blow your nose in publicAvoid doing it loudly at a table; otherwise fine
Pouring your own drink is rudeOnly a nicety in group settings; no one is offended
You must say "itadakimasu"A pleasant habit, not an obligation
Tattoos are offensive everywhereOnly an issue at some baths and pools, not on the street
Never pointMild. Pointing at a menu is how most ordering happens
You must use both hands for business cardsOnly if you are actually exchanging them

The pattern: Japan is a high-courtesy society, but it is also used to visitors. Good faith visibly offered covers almost every gap in technique.

Shrines and temples

  • Shrine gates (torii): a small bow before passing through is customary, not required.
  • Washing basin: rinse left hand, right hand, then pour into your left hand to rinse

your mouth. Don't drink from the ladle.

  • Offering: coin in the box, bow twice, clap twice, bow once at a shrine. At a temple,

no clapping.

  • Photography: fine outside, often forbidden inside halls. Look for the signs.
  • Dress: no requirement, but shoulders covered is a courtesy at major sites.

Getting the sequence wrong at a shrine is completely unremarkable. Taking flash photos of a Buddha statue after walking past three signs is not.

Queueing

Japan queues for everything, in a specific line, in order. Platform markings, bus stops, shop entrances, restaurant doors. Find the back of the line and join it rather than approaching the counter. If you cannot tell where the line is, ask with a sumimasen and a gesture.

The convenience store and the shop

  • Say nothing if you prefer; the whole transaction works wordlessly
  • Put your basket on the counter rather than handing items over
  • Declining a bag is normal: a small hand wave or daijōbu desu
  • Don't eat purchased food standing in front of the shop, unless there is a seating area

What actually makes people warm to you

Three small things, in order of effect:

  1. Trying a word of Japanese, however badly pronounced
  2. Noticing the system and following it — the line, the tray, the shoes
  3. Being quiet and unhurried in crowded places

That is the whole etiquette syllabus. Nobody expects fluency in the customs, and the effort is noticed far more than the accuracy.
