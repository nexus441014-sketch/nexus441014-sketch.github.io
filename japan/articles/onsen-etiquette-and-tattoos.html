Onsen Etiquette and Tattoos: What Actually Matters

Two things stop people using one of the best parts of a trip to Japan: not knowing the procedure, and having a tattoo. The first takes five minutes to learn. The second has workarounds nobody tells you about.

Three different things with similar rules

Onsen means the water comes from a hot spring. Sentō is a neighbourhood public bath on ordinary heated water. A hotel's daiyokujō is a large shared bath that may or may not be fed by a spring.

The etiquette below is the same in all three. The differences that matter to you are price, atmosphere, and whether tattoos are an issue — which varies by individual establishment rather than by category.

The procedure, in order

This is the whole thing. Nobody is watching you as closely as you fear.

  1. Shoes off at the entrance, into a locker or shelf.
  2. Pay, or show your room key if you are staying.
  3. Enter the correct changing room. Red noren curtain is usually women, blue usually

men, but the kanji is what counts: 女 women, 男 men. They sometimes swap the baths over between days, so check the characters rather than remembering yesterday's side.

  1. Undress completely in the changing room. All of it. Swimwear is not worn and is

not permitted.

  1. Take only the small towel into the bathing area. The large one stays in your basket

for drying off afterwards.

  1. Wash thoroughly, sitting down, at the shower stations before you get in the bath.

This is the rule that genuinely matters: the bath water is shared and is not for cleaning in. Rinse the stool and the area when you finish.

  1. Get in slowly. It is hotter than you expect.
  2. Do not put the small towel in the water. Fold it on your head, or rest it on the

edge. This is the thing visitors most often get wrong, and it is a hygiene rule rather than a quirk.

  1. Dry off before going back into the changing room, using the small towel first so

you do not flood the floor.

What actually matters, and what does not

Matters: washing before entering, keeping the towel out of the water, tying long hair up and out of the water, no phones or cameras anywhere in the changing or bathing area, and no loud conversation.

Does not matter nearly as much as people fear: being naked in front of strangers. Nobody looks. Standing around deciding what to do draws more attention than simply getting on with it.

A real one people miss: do not go in drunk, and do not go in straight after a large meal. Hot water plus alcohol causes a meaningful number of accidents in Japan every year. If you have heart or blood-pressure conditions, treat a very hot bath as exercise and check with a doctor first.

Tattoos: the honest situation

There is no national rule, and no rule you can rely on. Each establishment decides for itself, and the decision has nothing to do with how your tattoo looks or what it means to you — it comes from a domestic association between tattoos and organised crime.

The government has asked operators to be more accommodating to tattooed foreign visitors, and has suggested measures such as cover stickers and separate bathing times. That request is non-binding: it is a suggestion to industry bodies, not a law, and plenty of places have not changed. Refusals were common when it was issued and remain common enough that you cannot assume you will be fine.

So do not plan around hope. Plan around one of these four, in descending order of reliability:

1. Book a room with its own bath. A room with a private open-air bath removes the question completely. This is the reliable answer, it costs more, and for many people with tattoos it is the only way to have the experience at all. Worth budgeting one night for.

**2. Book a private bath (kashikiri-buro).** Many ryokan and some public facilities rent a whole bath by the hour, for couples, families, or anyone who wants privacy. Often bookable at check-in, sometimes free, sometimes a small charge. Ask when you book, not when you arrive.

3. Cover it. Waterproof cover stickers are sold in Japan and online, and some facilities keep them at reception. Workable for something small. Not workable for a sleeve, and applying one to a large piece and hoping is how people get asked to leave mid-bath.

4. Find a facility that permits tattoos. They exist and are increasingly easy to find, particularly in tourist and ski areas. Check the facility's own website, or ask your accommodation to phone and ask. Phoning is more reliable than a website, because websites are often out of date on this specific point.

Ask before you undress. Showing staff a photo on your phone and asking is a normal, undramatic conversation. Being asked to get out after you are already in the water is the outcome worth avoiding.

If you only do one

If the whole thing feels like too much, a private bath at a ryokan gives you the experience with none of the social uncertainty — no etiquette audience, no tattoo question, no deciding which curtain. It is a good way to do it once and find out whether you want the public version.

Ryokan have their own booking quirks, including being priced per person rather than per room, covered in where to stay in Japan. Rooms with private baths are a limited stock at most properties and go early, which puts them in the category of things to sort before you fly: see what you need to book before you arrive.
