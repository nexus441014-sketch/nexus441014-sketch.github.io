Where to Stay in Japan: Business Hotel, Ryokan, Hostel or Apartment

Japanese accommodation is categorised differently from what most visitors are used to, and the categories matter more than the star ratings. Picking the wrong type is the mistake; picking the wrong property within a type rarely is.

The rule that beats everything else

Distance to a station matters more than distance to the centre.

In most countries you pay for a central location because you walk from there. In Japanese cities you will take trains constantly, so the thing that shapes your days is how long it takes to get from your room to a platform. Eight minutes from a major interchange in an unremarkable neighbourhood beats a famous district with a fifteen-minute walk to a minor line, every time.

Look up your shortlisted property on a map, find the nearest station, and check which lines stop there. A property on a line you will actually use is worth more than one with a better photograph.

The five types

Business hotel. The default for most visitors, and the one they have usually never heard of. Rooms are small — genuinely small — but clean, quiet, reliably equipped, and almost always within a few minutes of a station. The bathroom is typically a moulded one-piece unit. You get a bed, a desk, good soundproofing and very little floor space.

For a trip where you are out all day and return to sleep, this is the correct answer and the best value in the country. For a trip where you want to spend afternoons in the room, it is the wrong one.

Ryokan. Not a hotel with tatami, but a different product with its own rules, and the one that catches people out:

  • It is usually priced per person, not per room. Two people in one room often costs

close to double what the headline number suggests.

  • Dinner and breakfast are frequently included and served at fixed times. Arriving at

eight in the evening can mean missing the meal you already paid for.

  • Check-in windows can be narrower than at a hotel, and so can check-out.

Stay in one for a night or two as an experience, and plan that day around the ryokan rather than fitting the ryokan around your day. Do not book one as a base for sightseeing.

Most have a shared bath, and many have private ones that can be booked by the hour. If you have tattoos, that distinction decides whether you can bathe at all: see onsen etiquette and tattoos.

Hostel and guesthouse. Japan's are unusually good: clean, well run, often with private rooms as well as dorms. Worth considering for solo travellers even outside the backpacker age range, and the staff are frequently the most useful local source you will meet.

Private apartment. Useful for groups and longer stays, especially with laundry. In Japan these are regulated: a legally operated private lodging has a registration number and the operator must display it. If a listing has no number, that is a reason to look elsewhere. Note too that many have no reception, so check-in is by lockbox or app, and there is nobody to hold your luggage.

Capsule hotel. Fine for one night, a late arrival, or a missed last train. Not a place to leave a suitcase open or spend an evening. Many are single-sex; some have no female floor at all.

What the listing will not tell you

Room size in square metres. This is the number that predicts whether the room will feel acceptable, and it is far more informative than the star rating. Find it in the listing details. If it is absent, assume small.

Whether there is a lift. Smaller buildings sometimes have none, or a very small one. With a large suitcase and a third-floor room, that is your evening.

Whether luggage can be left before check-in and after check-out. Larger hotels almost always will. Small guesthouses and unstaffed apartments often cannot. If your flight times do not match the check-in window, this decides your first and last day — or send the bags ahead instead, as in luggage forwarding in Japan.

Bed or futon. A tatami room may mean a futon laid on the floor. Many people sleep better on one than they expect; some do not. Know which you are booking.

Smoking rooms. Still exist in Japan in places where other countries have eliminated them. If it matters to you, filter for it explicitly rather than assuming.

When to book

For ordinary dates in large cities, a few weeks ahead is comfortable and prices are stable. For the domestic holiday weeks and the cherry blossom and autumn colour peaks, book months ahead: those are the periods when rooms genuinely run out rather than merely getting expensive. See what you need to book before you arrive.

Where to book

Compare at least two sources. The same room is frequently listed at different prices, and which one is cheaper is not consistent.

  • The property's own site. Often matches or beats the platforms, and Japanese chain

business hotels frequently have a members' rate. Worth a look for a stay of several nights.

  • An international platform. English interface, foreign cards, and a cancellation

policy you can read. Klook's hotel listings cover Japan alongside the activities side of the site, which is convenient if you are already booking tickets there.

Whichever you use, read the cancellation terms before you pay. Non-refundable rates save a modest amount and remove all flexibility, which is a poor trade on a first trip to a country where you cannot yet predict your own pace.

The honest default

Business hotels near interchange stations for most nights, one ryokan night if the itinerary has room for it, and a hostel or apartment if you are travelling solo or in a group respectively. That combination suits most first trips better than booking the same type throughout.
