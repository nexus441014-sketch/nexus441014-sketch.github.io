Is Japan Safe? What Actually Goes Wrong

Violent crime is not the thing to plan for. Heat, typhoons, earthquakes, bicycles and one specific kind of bar are. None of it should stop you going — but knowing which risk is which is worth ten minutes.

The part everyone asks about

Japan is one of the safest countries you can travel in for street crime. Walking alone at night in a major city is normal. Leaving a bag on a café seat while you order is normal. Dropped wallets are frequently handed in to police boxes and returned.

You should not spend your planning time on muggings. Spend it on the list below.

Heat is the number one underestimated risk

A Japanese summer is humid in a way that catches people out, and the worst of it now runs well into September. Heatstroke sends people to hospital every year, including visitors walking between temples at 2pm.

Treat midday in summer as indoor time. Shrines at 8am, museums and department stores at 1pm, back outside after 5pm. Carry water and refill it; vending machines and convenience stores are everywhere.

Typhoons: plan the schedule, not the gear

Typhoon season roughly spans August to October. A direct hit is unlikely on any single trip, but a passing storm closing rail lines for a day is not.

What this means practically:

  • Don't put a tight flight connection on the last day of a late-summer trip
  • Book refundable accommodation for the back half of the trip if you can
  • Watch the forecast three days out, and move a travel day rather than ride it out
  • Shinkansen services suspend pre-emptively and resume quickly; this is a delay, not a

crisis

Earthquakes: two rules

Small tremors are common and nothing happens. For anything larger, the whole of the advice you need is:

  1. Stay inside and get under something sturdy. Do not run outside — falling glass and

tiles are the danger.

  1. Follow staff and crowds. Buildings are built for this, and hotel and station staff

are drilled on it.

Enable the official earthquake alerts on your phone if you can — they often give a few seconds' warning, which is enough to step away from a window.

If you are near the coast and feel a long, rolling quake, move to higher ground without waiting to be told. This is the one situation where you act first.

The one genuine scam to know about

In a small number of nightlife districts — Kabukichō in Tokyo and parts of Roppongi are the usual names — touts on the street invite foreign visitors into bars that then produce a bill many times what was implied, sometimes with a card already run.

The rule is simple and absolute: never go anywhere with someone who approached you on the street. Legitimate bars in Japan do not need to recruit outside. If you want a bar, walk in off your own research or ask your accommodation.

Crowded trains

Groping on packed commuter trains is a real and reported problem. Many operators run women-only carriages at peak times, marked in pink on the platform floor and on the car itself.

If something happens, grabbing the offender's wrist and saying "yamete" (stop) loudly is the standard response, and other passengers and station staff do intervene.

Things that are safe but feel unsafe

  • Walking home at 1am in a residential area. Normal.
  • Cash. Carrying a reasonable amount is standard practice here, because

cash is still needed in places.

  • Leaving luggage in a station coin locker. Routine, and

covered here.

  • Solo female travel. Common and well-supported, with the train caveat above.
  • Tap water. Drinkable nationwide.

Things that feel safe but are not

  • Stepping sideways on a pavement. Bicycles ride on pavements legally and quietly.
  • Crossing a quiet street on red. Drivers genuinely do not expect it.
  • Bringing your usual medication. Some common Western drugs are restricted here —

check before you fly.

  • Travelling uninsured. There is no reciprocal healthcare arrangement for visitors, and

you will be billed.

The numbers to have in your phone

  • 119 — ambulance and fire
  • 110 — police
  • Japan Visitor Hotline (JNTO) — 050-3816-2787 (from overseas +81-50-3816-2787).

Run by the national tourism organisation, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, in English, Chinese and Korean, for emergencies and general tourist information

Save those three before you fly. That is the whole of your safety preparation, plus a hat in summer and a flexible schedule in autumn.

Official sources

  • Japan Meteorological Agency — earthquake, tsunami, typhoon and weather warnings
  • Safety tips app (JNTO) — the official multilingual alert app for visitors
  • Japan Visitor Hotline (JNTO) — the official assistance line, with its hours and

languages

  • Japan National Tourism Organization — official travel information
Weather and seismic conditions change constantly, and emergency guidance is issued by the authorities at the time. Follow the meteorological agency and local instructions over anything written here in advance.
