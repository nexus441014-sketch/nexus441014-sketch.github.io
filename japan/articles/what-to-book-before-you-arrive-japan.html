What You Need to Book Before You Arrive in Japan

Turning up and buying a ticket works for most of Japan. For a specific and predictable set of things, it does not work at all—and the ones that catch people out are rarely the ones they worried about.

The pattern nobody warns you about

Japan is full of places you can walk into. Temples, shrines, most museums, almost every restaurant, nearly all shops. The relaxed default makes the exceptions more jarring: a handful of things run on timed-entry slots, advance-only sales, or outright lotteries, and for those, showing up means not getting in.

The exceptions are predictable. Work through them once while you are still planning, and you will not meet any of them as a surprise.

Book well ahead: advance-only or lottery

These are not "buy at the door" experiences, and some are not even first-come-first-served.

Timed-entry art and museum experiences. Several of Japan's best-known attractions sell slots rather than tickets, with no counter sales at all. Methods and sale dates change, so go to the venue's own site rather than a summary:

  • teamLab — timed slots, sold online
  • Ghibli Museum, Mitaka — advance only, with its own

sale process

  • Ghibli Park — separate from the museum, separate rules

Sumo tournaments. Only a handful of tournaments run each year, each in a specific city over a fixed fortnight. If your dates coincide, that is luck; if you want good seats, you need to act when sales open. Official information and the tournament calendar: Japan Sumo Association.

Kabuki and other traditional theatre. Performances run in seasons, and popular programmes sell through. Single-act tickets are often easier than full programmes. Official: Kabuki Web.

Observation decks at sunset. The decks themselves are usually fine on the day. The hour either side of sunset is the slot everyone wants, and at the popular ones it goes first. Shibuya Sky is the clearest example in Tokyo.

Book a few weeks ahead: seasonal and capacity-limited

Accommodation during the big domestic travel weeks. There are periods when the whole country travels at once. Rooms do not merely get expensive, they run out, including in cities you assumed were large enough to absorb it.

Anything during cherry blossom or autumn colour peak in the famous spots. The windows are short, the dates move year to year, and demand is enormous.

Highly rated small restaurants. A ten-seat counter cannot absorb walk-ins. Many take reservations only by phone, in Japanese, or only through a hotel concierge. If a specific restaurant is a goal of the trip, solve it before you fly, not on the day.

Rail seat reservations on long legs during holiday periods. Covered in do you need a Japan Rail Pass: the pass question and the seat question are separate, and the seat one matters more when you have luggage or are travelling as a group.

Do not bother booking ahead

Equally useful to know, because this is where people waste money and flexibility:

  • Temples and shrines. Walk in. A handful of special interiors have separate tickets,

sold there.

  • Most museums and galleries. Buy at the door.
  • Ordinary restaurants. Including very good ones. Lunch almost never needs a booking.
  • Day-to-day transport. IC cards

cover it with no planning at all.

  • Most guided day tours. See [when a day tour is worth

it](/articles/day-tours-in-japan-worth-it.html) — the answer is often "not here".

Over-booking a Japan trip is a real failure mode. A schedule with no slack cannot absorb the small discoveries that are the actual reason to be there.

The practical sequence

  1. Fix your dates, then check them against the domestic holiday periods.
  2. Book accommodation for those dates if they overlap one.
  3. List the three experiences you would genuinely be disappointed to miss.
  4. Check each one's official site for how it sells tickets. Put the sale dates in your

calendar if they are in the future.

  1. Leave everything else unbooked.

Three is a deliberate number. Beyond that, you are building an itinerary that will make you rush past the things you came to see.

When you cannot read the booking page

Some official booking flows are Japanese-only or reject foreign cards. Options, in order: ask your accommodation to help, use the venue's English page if one exists, or use a reseller that handles international payment. A reseller costs more than the official channel; it is worth it only when the official channel does not work for you.
