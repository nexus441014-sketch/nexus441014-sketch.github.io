## The reputation is out of date, but not wrong

Cards, IC cards and phone payment work across chain stores, convenience stores, major
restaurants, hotels, department stores and most transport in urban Japan. A visitor
sticking to that world could nearly manage without yen.

Nearly. The exceptions are specific, and they tend to appear at inconvenient moments.

## Where you will still want cash

- **Small independent restaurants**, especially older ones and anywhere with a ticket
  machine at the door. Some ticket machines take only coins and notes.
- **Shrines and temples**: entry, charms and offerings are cash matters.
- **Local buses** in smaller cities and rural areas.
- **Rural and mountain areas** generally, where acceptance thins out quickly once you
  leave the main stations.
- **Markets and festival stalls.**
- **Some coin lockers**, although IC cards increasingly cover these.
- **Small accommodation**, such as family-run guesthouses.

The pattern: the smaller and older the business, and the further from a major station,
the more likely it is cash only.

## How much to carry

Enough for a day of small purchases, topped up when it runs low, rather than a large
amount withdrawn once. Japan is unusually safe for carrying cash, but a lost wallet is
still a lost wallet.

A useful habit: break large notes at a convenience store early. Small independent
businesses may struggle with a 10,000 yen note for a small purchase, and handing one over
for a cheap item is mildly awkward.

## Getting yen

**Convenience store ATMs** are the practical answer for most visitors. They are
everywhere, available long hours, have English interfaces, and generally accept foreign
cards — which is not true of every bank ATM in Japan.

Before you travel, check three things with your own bank: whether your card works abroad
at all, what the foreign transaction fee is, and whether there is a daily withdrawal
limit that would force repeated trips.

**Do not rely on airport currency exchange as your plan.** It works, but it is rarely the
best rate, and you will want an ATM routine anyway for a trip of any length.

## Cards: what to expect

- **Contactless is widespread** but not universal, and some terminals want the chip.
- **You may be asked to sign** rather than enter a PIN. Know your PIN regardless.
- **Splitting a bill across several cards** is often not possible in smaller restaurants.
  Decide how you are splitting before you ask.
- **Tell your bank you are travelling** if it is the kind that blocks unexpected foreign
  transactions.

## Tipping

There is no tipping in Japan, and attempting it causes confusion rather than pleasure.
The price is the price. This genuinely simplifies the cash question: you never need to
keep small notes aside for gratuities.

## The practical setup

1. One card that works abroad, with the foreign transaction fee known in advance.
2. A second card from a different network, kept separately, in case the first fails.
3. An [IC card](/articles/ic-cards-suica-pasmo-explained.html) for transport and small
   purchases.
4. A modest amount of cash, topped up at convenience store ATMs as needed.

That combination handles essentially every situation a visitor meets.

## One thing that changed recently

Tax-free shopping no longer works the way most older guides describe. Since the system
changed, you pay the full price including tax at the register and claim the refund when
you leave the country. That affects how much you spend on the day, so it belongs in your
cash planning. See [tax-free shopping in
Japan](/articles/tax-free-shopping-in-japan.html).

> Acceptance, fees and ATM availability vary by region and provider, and change. Check
> with your own bank before relying on any card abroad.
