## What an IC card actually is

A prepaid card you top up with cash, then tap on a reader. It works as your ticket on
trains, subways and buses across most of urban Japan, and it also pays at convenience
stores, vending machines, lockers and many small shops.

The important part for a visitor: **you stop buying individual tickets.** No working out
the fare to your destination from a wall map before you can board. You tap in, tap out,
and the correct fare is deducted.

Suica and Pasmo are the two best-known names, issued by different operators in the Tokyo
area. There are regional equivalents elsewhere. For travel purposes they are broadly
interchangeable across the major networks, so the one you happen to get is rarely worth
agonising over.

## Why it matters more than it sounds

- **Coin luggage lockers** at stations mostly take IC cards, and hunting for the right
  coins with a suitcase is a bad way to spend fifteen minutes.
- **Buses** in many cities expect either exact cash or an IC tap. The tap is far easier
  than working out a zone fare in coins.
- **Transfers between operators** happen invisibly. Japanese cities mix several rail
  companies, and paper tickets make you aware of every boundary. An IC card does not.

## Physical card or phone

**On your phone** is better if your device supports it. There is nothing to collect, no
deposit, and you can top up from the phone instead of finding a machine. Compatibility
depends on your phone's hardware and region, so check before you rely on it.

**A physical card** is the fallback, and works for everyone. Availability of cards for
visitors has fluctuated in recent years, including periods where ordinary cards were not
being sold to anyone and only visitor-specific versions were available. Check the current
situation close to your travel date rather than assuming.

Either way, set this up at the airport or your first station. Doing it on day one is what
makes the rest of the trip smooth.

## How topping up works

Top up at the machines in any station, and at convenience store registers. Cash is always
accepted; card top-up is not universal, so do not count on it.

Keep a working balance rather than topping up constantly. Running out at a ticket gate at
rush hour, with a queue behind you, is the one genuinely stressful IC card experience, and
it is entirely avoidable.

## What it does not cover

- **Long-distance express services** generally need their own ticket on top. The IC card
  is for ordinary travel, not for the fast intercity network.
- **Some rural lines and some buses** remain cash-only. Carry some cash for exactly this
  reason, as covered in [cash or card in Japan](/articles/cash-or-card-in-japan.html).
- **Not every shop.** Acceptance is wide but not total, especially for small independent
  businesses.

## Leaving the country

Physical cards carry a deposit and may hold a remaining balance. Refund procedures exist
but vary by issuer and region, and the refund can involve a handling fee that makes
reclaiming a small balance pointless.

The practical approach: as the trip ends, stop topping up and spend the balance down at
convenience stores. If you expect to return to Japan, keep the card. Validity periods
apply, so check the issuer's terms if it will sit in a drawer for years.

## The one-line version

Get one in your first hour, keep a few thousand yen on it, spend it down before you fly
home, and keep some cash for the places that still do not take it.
